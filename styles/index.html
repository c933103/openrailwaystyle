    A world built around rail

    A station-first adaptation of Open Railway Styles, started at Hack4Rail. The background keeps roads and settlements subdued so railway infrastructure stands out.

    Reading the map

    Regional views show a spaced-out selection of major stations. Smaller stations appear as you zoom in; individual station markers appear at local scale. Major stations receive priority according to the provider’s mapped route importance, which is not a passenger-volume ranking. Coverage and station ranking depend on OpenStreetMap completeness.

    Speed colours use the provider’s numeric limit in km/h. Where directions differ, this is the preferred-direction limit, or the larger directional limit when no preferred direction is mapped. Track labels retain original units: bare numbers are km/h; mph is written explicitly. “A / B” means forward / backward; “A (B)” means preferred / opposite direction. A dash means missing data. These directions refer to the underlying OSM way.

    Grey means no numeric speed is available. Railways under construction are drawn with long dashes, proposed ones with spaced round dots, disused ones with dash-dot lines, and abandoned or removed ones with sparse pairs of short dashes; they are not presented as operating lines. Construction shows at every zoom, proposals from zoom 5 and former lines from zoom 7. In the speed view they take the colour of their recorded (planned or former) limit where one is mapped. Bridges (dark edges) and tunnels (pale dashed core or faded line) are marked in every view from zoom 7. Recorded infrastructure limits are not train running speeds or a timetable.

    In the Power view the hue is the current type (DC, or AC by frequency: 16.7, 25, 50 or 60 Hz) and the shade is the voltage, darker being higher, since a train needs both to match. The Train control view colours each line by its first recorded train protection or control system: related systems share a hue (for example ETCS with China’s ETCS-derived CTCS and Korea’s KTCS; PZB with LZB; AWS with TPWS), and the shade shows how advanced a system is, from warning only, through spot transmission and continuous transmission, to radio-based movement authority. Clicking a line gives every recorded system and notes on compatibility. The Gauge view uses a continuous scale: gauges within about 10 mm of a common gauge (1432 and 1435 mm, 1520 and 1524 mm, 1668 and 1676 mm) share its colour, and a dual-gauge track is split lengthwise, one half in each gauge’s colour. The Loading gauge view colours each line by the height above rail of its loading gauge’s static profile, so equal envelopes match across regions whatever they are called (for example GC and G2 at 4.65 m, GA to GB2 at 4.32 m, Russia’s T at 5.30 m, AAR Plate C at 4.72 m); Britain’s W gauges share one height and differ in the containers they clear, so they form their own ladder from W6A to W12. Where several gauges are listed for a line, the largest is shown. Zoomed out, the overview is coloured from a list of loading gauges by OpenStreetMap way, refreshed three times a week; values drawn in the same colour share a legend row. From zoom 10 each view writes its values along the tracks, as the speed view does; the legends name exactly the values in view. These views show only what is recorded in OpenStreetMap; many lines have no train protection tagged. From zoom 14 the Infrastructure view shows how many running tracks lie side by side, as a boxed number on the middle track of each group. It is counted from the map itself, since OpenStreetMap maps each track separately: running tracks (not tagged as sidings, yards, spurs or crossovers) that keep alongside each other, each within 30 m of the next, form a group; between two tunnel tracks, 12 m, or 35 m for the two tracks of the same named line (twin-bore tunnels). Tracks that cut across from one to another, such as crossovers, are not counted. The count is the most common one over about 300 m along the line, so it changes where tracks join or leave but not for a brief crossover, and it is always made from the same detailed railway tiles, so it is the same at every zoom. Inside station areas (as mapped around each station's elements, whatever the station's size) running tracks are not labelled, as tracks there spread around platforms. Tracks in tunnels count with those beside them on the surface or on viaducts (a line quadrupled with one pair underground is one group of four), with the 12 m and 35 m gaps only between two tunnel tracks; a group wholly in tunnels has a grey-blue badge. Stations have an amber badge of their own: every track at the station, sidings included, on every level. It reflects how completely the tracks are mapped, and parallel lines of different railways count together.

    Station labels give priority to heavy rail, then metro, light rail, people movers and trams, then former stations. The ⊞ button shows more detail: pressed once, it draws the next zoom level at half size; pressed again, two zoom levels further in at a quarter size, for the densest view (for example level crossings, which appear from zoom 15, while seeing an area as wide as at zoom 13); a third press returns to normal. Its tooltip gives the scale the map is drawn at (100%, 50% or 25%). The ✎ button opens drawing tools (points, lines, smooth curves and areas, in a chosen colour and line style); a curve leaves and joins straight stretches without a corner, the small dot in the middle of each segment adds a point there, and with the tools closed, clicking a drawn line gives its elevation profile. Drawings stay in this browser and can be saved or opened as GeoJSON files. The 📏 button measures distances along several segments, with the height difference and gradient between the ends, or the radius of a curve from three or more points clicked along it. Heights come from the terrain tiles: ground or seabed level, not the track on a bridge or in a tunnel. Under the scale bar are the coordinates under the cursor and the zoom (Display options can hide them).

    Names and terrain

    One language setting applies to map, station and line labels. Only names recorded in the source data are shown; nothing is automatically translated or transliterated. When no name is recorded in the chosen language, a related recorded name is used where one exists, such as the other Chinese script or English, and otherwise the local name.

    Station translations are requested for the current view. Snapshot railways retain multilingual OSM tags; the current operating-line tile provider usually supplies only native names. The shared setting cannot recover translations omitted from a source. Search results use the names returned by the search service.

    Brown contours show land elevation; blue contours show negative elevation below sea level. Labels are signed metres. Land intervals become finer as you zoom: 200 m at zoom 7, 100 m at 9, 50 m at 11, 20 m at 13, and 10 m at 15. Seabed contours are 50 m at zooms 5–8 (down to 200 m), 20 m at 9–10 and 10 m from zoom 11. Heavier index contours are labelled. The elevation data holds seabed depths only up to zoom 10, so closer seabed contours follow that coarser data; land detail continues to zoom 15. On the globe, beyond 85.05° N and S where the map's tiles end, relief and contours come from NOAA ETOPO 2022 (about 1.85 km resolution).

    Roads, cycling paths and walking trails remain visible beneath the railway network. Protected areas, military grounds, religious institutions and heritage sites provide context for drawing proposals. Purple boundaries show Indigenous territories where mapped as non-administrative jurisdictions, separately from administrative and conservation boundaries. The map does not establish legal boundaries or permission to build.

    Infrastructure view marks level crossings as dots from zoom 5 (dark brown for road crossings, light brown for pedestrian ones), with × symbols and their mapped equipment from zoom 15; the dots come from a worldwide OSM table refreshed weekly: snapshot date and counts; download the ODbL table. It marks explicitly tagged street-running track with an ochre roadbed. Street running is refreshed weekly from OSM: snapshot date and query; download the ODbL extract. It is not inferred from every tramway or a nearby road.

    Sources

    Railway tiles: OpenRailwayMap vector. Station search: OpenRailwayMap public API. Regional planned, construction and former lines: published OSM snapshot, extracted with Overpass (FOSSGIS); download the ODbL database. Relief: Mapzen Terrain Tiles / AWS, including NOAA ETOPO1 seabed data. Base map: En Liberté. Map data: © OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL. Name regions: Natural Earth, timezone-boundary-builder (OSM, ODbL) and OSM boundary relations and geoBoundaries (Myanmar Analytics Project, CC BY 4.0; Royal Thai Survey Department / OCHA ROAP, CC BY 3.0 IGO). Renderer: MapLibre.

    This public map uses community-hosted services. Data availability and updates follow those providers. No account or API key is required. Search terms are sent to OpenRailwayMap only when submitted. Panning loads planned and former railways from files published with this site; your viewport is not sent to Overpass. The snapshot date is recorded in its manifest. Detail tiles at zoom 12 and above follow OpenRailwayMap updates. Shading and contours are derived from elevation, including seabed where recorded; they are not a nautical chart.

    Other transport and major destinations use the existing OpenMapTiles basemap: airports, bus/coach and ferry terminals, cable car stations, hospitals, campuses, shopping centres, government/community facilities and visitor attractions. Shaded industrial, commercial and other facility grounds show potential trip destinations, not passenger volumes. At street detail, station panels list nearby transport within 500 m straight-line distance; this does not confirm a walkable interchange.
