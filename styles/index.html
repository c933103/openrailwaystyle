    Getting started

    Search for a station, railway facility or place with the box at the top of the panel. The view buttons colour the railways by infrastructure, maximum speed, power, train control, gauge, loading gauge, axle load or owner, or show the urban rail services (metro, light rail, tram and commuter rail lines) along the tracks they run on; Map, Satellite, Hybrid and Carto choose the background. Click a station, line, level crossing or transport facility to inspect it; its panel links to the object on OpenStreetMap.

    At the foot of the panel: the link button copies a link to the current view, the satellite button copies the GPS coordinates of the map centre, the gear opens the settings (what is drawn, the coordinate readout and the automatic globe), and ? opens this page. Beside the map: compass, zoom, your location, ⊞ (more detail), ✎ (drawing), 📏 (measuring) and the globe/flat-map switch. With the map focused, the arrow keys pan the map and Shift with an arrow turns or tilts it.

    Railway Atlas is a worldwide railway map with prominent stations, infrastructure and service routes. Subdued roads and settlements, land and seabed relief, and drawing and measurement tools provide context for exploring railways.

    Reading the map

    Major stations are ranked by the provider’s mapped route importance, which is not a passenger-volume ranking. Coverage and station ranking depend on OpenStreetMap completeness.

    Speed colours use the provider’s numeric limit in km/h. Where directions differ, this is the preferred-direction limit, or the larger directional limit when no preferred direction is mapped. Track labels retain original units: bare numbers are km/h; mph is written explicitly. “A / B” means forward / backward; “A (B)” means preferred / opposite direction. A dash means missing data. These directions refer to the underlying OSM way.

    Grey means no numeric speed is available. Railways under construction are drawn with long dashes, proposed ones with spaced round dots, disused ones with dash-dot lines, and abandoned or removed ones with sparse pairs of short dashes; they are not presented as operating lines. In the speed view they take the colour of their recorded (planned or former) limit where one is mapped. Bridges (dark edges) and tunnels (pale dashed core or faded line) are marked in every view. Recorded infrastructure limits are not train running speeds or a timetable.

    The Power view also shows mapped railway electricity, locomotive fuel, coal and steam-water supplies, marked E/F/C/W; hollow markers identify planned or former facilities when those are enabled. For tracks the hue is the current type (DC, or AC by frequency: 16.7, 25, 50 or 60 Hz) and the shade is the voltage, darker being higher, since a train needs both to match. The Train control view splits each line into parallel colour bands for its recorded train protection or control systems: related systems share a hue (for example ETCS with China’s ETCS-derived CTCS and Korea’s KTCS; PZB with LZB; AWS with TPWS), and the shade shows how advanced a system is, from warning only, through spot transmission and continuous transmission, to radio-based movement authority. Short system names in the legend and line details show full explanations on hover or when tapped. Clicking a line gives every recorded system. The Gauge view uses a continuous scale: gauges within about 10 mm of a common gauge (1432 and 1435 mm, 1520 and 1524 mm, 1668 and 1676 mm) share its colour, and a dual-gauge track is split lengthwise, one half in each gauge’s colour. The Loading gauge view colours each line by the height above rail of its loading gauge’s static profile, so equal envelopes match across regions whatever they are called (for example GC and G2 at 4.65 m, GA to GB2 at 4.32 m, Russia’s T at 5.30 m, AAR Plate C at 4.72 m); Britain’s W gauges share one height and differ in the containers they clear, so they form their own ladder from W6A to W12. Where several gauges are listed for a line, the largest is shown. The Owner view gives each infrastructure owner recorded in OpenStreetMap its own colour, the same everywhere; the owner is not always the operator. The legends name the values in view. Train control keeps a separate entry for each system; other views group values drawn in the same colour. These views show only what is recorded in OpenStreetMap; many lines have no train protection tagged. The Infrastructure view shows how many running tracks lie side by side as a boxed number, counted from the tracks mapped in OpenStreetMap; sidings, yards, spurs and crossovers are not counted, and a group wholly in tunnels has a grey-blue badge. Stations have an amber badge of their own: every track at the station, sidings included, on every level, for each kind of railway it serves. Both reflect how completely the tracks are mapped; main lines of different railways count together, while light rail and trams are counted on their own. The settings can hide the boxes.

    The Map, Satellite and Hybrid buttons choose the background: the drawn map, satellite imagery alone, or the imagery under the railways and stations of the chosen view. The imagery is 10 m per pixel, so single tracks are not visible in it. A station's panel lists its next rail departures from published timetables, with live times and delays where the operator publishes real-time data (GTFS-RT), and links to plan a journey from or to it. The location button shows your position and follows it as you move; the position is used only in your browser. The atlas can be installed as an app from the browser menu.

    The ⊞ button shows more detail in the same space, drawn at half and then a quarter size; a third press returns to normal, and its tooltip gives the scale. The ✎ button opens drawing tools (points, lines, smooth curves and areas, in a chosen colour and line style); a curve leaves and joins straight stretches without a corner, the small dot in the middle of each segment adds a point there, and with the tools closed, clicking a drawn line gives its elevation profile. Drawings stay in this browser and can be saved or opened as GeoJSON files. The 📏 button measures distances along several segments, with the height difference and gradient between the ends, or the radius of a curve from three or more points clicked along it. Heights are ground or seabed level, not the track on a bridge or in a tunnel. Under the scale bar are the coordinates under the cursor and the zoom (the settings can hide them).

    Names and terrain

    One language setting applies to map, station and line labels. Only names recorded in the source data are shown; nothing is automatically translated or transliterated. When no name is recorded in the chosen language, a related recorded name is used where one exists, such as the other Chinese script or English, and otherwise the local name.

    Station translations are requested for the current view. Snapshot railways retain multilingual OSM tags; the current operating-line tile provider usually supplies only native names. The shared setting cannot recover translations omitted from a source. Search results use the names returned by the search service.

    Brown contours show land elevation; blue contours show negative elevation below sea level. Labels are signed metres. Heavier index contours are labelled. Seabed depth data is much coarser than land elevation. Around the poles on the globe, relief and contours come from NOAA ETOPO 2022 (about 1.85 km resolution).

    Roads, cycling paths and walking trails remain visible beneath the railway network. Protected areas, military grounds, religious institutions and heritage sites provide context for drawing proposals. Purple boundaries show Indigenous territories where mapped as non-administrative jurisdictions, separately from administrative and conservation boundaries. The map does not establish legal boundaries or permission to build.

    Infrastructure view marks level crossings (dark brown for road crossings, light brown for pedestrian ones), with their mapped equipment; they come from a worldwide OSM table refreshed weekly: snapshot date and counts; download the ODbL table. It marks explicitly tagged street-running track with an ochre roadbed. Street running is refreshed weekly from OSM: snapshot date and query; download the ODbL extract. It is not inferred from every tramway or a nearby road.

    Zoom in on Infrastructure view for platforms and their boarding edges. Platform numbers and edge references appear from zoom 17; from zoom 19 an edge label pairs its reference with the length of the complete mapped boarding edge, in metres or feet. Platform numbers and lengths can take a moment to load. Signals (purple) appear from zoom 13 in Infrastructure and Train control, with references from zoom 16. Mapped train or subway station entrances (teal) appear from zoom 16; click them for mapped details and an OpenStreetMap link. Signal markers identify their location, not their current aspect. Coverage depends on the provider’s mapped data. Value labels hides their text while keeping the mapped shapes and markers.

    Carto background: OpenStreetMap Standard, under the OSMF tile usage policy. Report a map issue.

    Sources

    Railway tiles: OpenRailwayMap vector. Station search: OpenRailwayMap public API; place search: Nominatim (OpenStreetMap data). Departures and journey planning: Transitous (timetable sources and licences). Regional planned, construction and former lines: published OSM snapshot, extracted with Overpass (FOSSGIS); download the ODbL database. Relief: Mapzen Terrain Tiles / AWS, including NOAA ETOPO1 seabed data. Base map: En Liberté. Satellite imagery: EOxCloudless https://cloudless.eox.at by EOX IT Services GmbH (Contains modified Copernicus Sentinel data 2025), CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. Map data: © OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL. Name regions: Natural Earth, timezone-boundary-builder (OSM, ODbL) and OSM boundary relations and geoBoundaries (Myanmar Analytics Project, CC BY 4.0; Royal Thai Survey Department / OCHA ROAP, CC BY 3.0 IGO). Renderer: MapLibre.

    Project origins: Railway Atlas began as a fork of Open Railway Styles, the Hack4Rail 2025 project. Atlas's current application and worldwide data pipeline are developed in this repository.

    This public map uses community-hosted services. Data availability and updates follow those providers. No account or API key is required. Search terms are sent to OpenRailwayMap and to OpenStreetMap's Nominatim geocoder only when submitted. Opening a station sends its position to Transitous for its departures. Opening a level crossing asks the OpenStreetMap API for its tags. Panning loads planned and former railways from files published with this site; your viewport is not sent to Overpass. The snapshot date is recorded in its manifest. Detail tiles follow OpenRailwayMap updates. Shading and contours are derived from elevation, including seabed where recorded; they are not a nautical chart.

    Other transport and major destinations use the existing OpenMapTiles basemap: airports, bus/coach and ferry terminals, cable car stations, hospitals, campuses, shopping centres, government/community facilities and visitor attractions. Shaded industrial, commercial and other facility grounds show potential trip destinations, not passenger volumes. Station panels list nearby transport within 500 m straight-line distance; this does not confirm a walkable interchange.
